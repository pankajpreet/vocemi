When the phone rings and nobody can pick up, a small business has four realistic options: let it go to **voicemail**, pay a **live answering service**, **hire or reassign staff**, or use an **AI receptionist**. Each has a place. The right choice depends less on the technology and more on what your callers want and what happens after the call.

## The quick comparison

| | Voicemail | Live answering service | In-house staff | AI receptionist |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Answers every call** | No | Usually, within staffing limits | Only when free | Yes, several at once |
| **Hours** | 24/7, but nobody answers | Often 24/7 | Business hours | 24/7 |
| **Can book appointments** | No | Sometimes, with access to your calendar | Yes | Yes, inside rules you set |
| **Knows your business** | No | Script-level | Deeply | What you approve, consistently |
| **Handles judgment calls** | No | Limited | Yes | Should hand off to a person |
| **Cost shape** | Free | Per minute or per call | Salary | Setup plus monthly |

## Voicemail

**Good for:** very low call volume, callers who already know you, and calls that can wait.

**Where it falls short:** many callers won't leave a message, and those who do are waiting on your callback. One provider's data on [16.7 million missed calls](https://www.quo.com/blog/small-business-callback-statistics/) found that 69% didn't get a callback within 48 hours. Voicemail only works if someone reliably calls back quickly.

**Keep it if:** you rarely miss calls, and missed callers hear back within an hour or two.

## Live answering service

**Good for:** taking messages, basic screening, and giving callers a human voice after hours.

**Where it falls short:** operators handle many businesses at once, so they usually work from a short script. Many can take a message but can't book, answer detailed questions, or update your systems. Busy periods can mean hold times. Per-minute pricing also makes long or frequent calls expensive.

**Choose it if:** your callers mostly need reassurance and a message passed on, and you're comfortable with script-level answers.

## In-house staff

**Good for:** complex, high-value or sensitive calls where judgment and relationships matter.

**Where it falls short:** people can only take one call at a time, and they're often busy with the customer in front of them. They also need breaks and cover. The calls that get missed are often the routine ones that interrupt everything else.

**Choose it if:** your calls need judgment most of the time. Even then, consider taking the routine calls off your team's plate so they can focus on the calls that need them.

## AI receptionist

**Good for:** routine, repeatable calls such as hours, pricing you've approved, availability, booking, rescheduling and intake. It answers immediately, day or night, and several calls can be handled at once.

**Where it falls short:** it should not be making judgment calls. Good setups have clear limits: it answers only from information you approve, books only inside your rules, and sends emergencies, complaints and unusual requests to a person. Voice models improved a lot in 2026, including agents that can [listen while they talk](/blog/full-duplex-voice-ai-business-calls). But a badly scoped AI receptionist is still worse than a good voicemail.

**Choose it if:** a large share of your calls are routine, you're missing calls at predictable times, and you'd rather your team spend their time on calls that need a person.

## Five questions to decide

1. **What do your callers actually want?** Review 20 recent calls. If most are booking and basic questions, automation fits. If most need judgment, it doesn't.
2. **When are you missing calls?** Our guide to [measuring your missed-call rate](/blog/missed-call-statistics) takes about a week.
3. **What should happen after the call?** A message in an inbox, a booking in your calendar, or a record in your CRM? The more that needs to happen, the less voicemail or a message service helps.
4. **What must never be automated?** List the call types that always go to a person. Every option above should respect that list.
5. **Can you try it first?** Test any option with realistic calls before committing: a messy booking, a price question, an angry caller.

## Mixing options is normal

Many businesses end up with a combination. Staff answer when they can. An AI receptionist picks up overflow and after-hours calls. And complex calls go to a person with a written summary so the caller doesn't have to repeat themselves. That's how we usually recommend starting: one workflow, one set of rules, and a clear handoff.
